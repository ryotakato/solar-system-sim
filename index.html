About this demonstration

Reference frames

A reference frame is the place we measure positions from. Every view here comes from the same planetary positions: position seen from X = position of planet − position of X. The planets do exactly the same thing in every view; only our viewpoint changes.

Retrograde motion

Planets usually drift steadily eastward against the stars. Every so often a planet seems to slow down, move backwards for a while, then carry on. This apparent backward motion is called retrograde motion.

Why loops appear from Earth

Earth is moving too. When Earth overtakes a slower outer planet such as Mars, the planet appears to slip backwards, just as a slower car seems to go backwards when you pass it. Add the planet's own motion and the path becomes a loop.

Why the Sun-centred paths look simpler

Seen from the Sun, each path involves only one motion, the planet's own orbit, so in this model each path is a single circle. Seen from Earth, every path combines two motions: the planet's and Earth's.

Epicycles: the historical description

Before the Sun-centred model, astronomers described these loops with circles on circles: the planet moves on a small circle (the epicycle) whose centre travels around Earth on a larger circle (the deferent). In this simplified model, one deferent plus one epicycle reproduces the Earth-centred paths exactly. They are the same motion, described in two ways. Historical models such as Ptolemy's added further refinements (eccentrics and equants) that are not shown here, and the true distances to the planets were not known at the time.

What the comparison does — and does not — show

Every reference frame is mathematically valid, so visual simplicity alone does not prove which cosmology is right. The case for the Sun-centred picture rests on a wider body of evidence, including telescope observations such as the full set of phases of Venus, Kepler's laws, Newton's theory of gravity, and measurements of stellar aberration and parallax.

Simplifications in this model

Orbits are drawn as circles in a single plane, using realistic average radii and periods. Real orbits are ellipses, slightly tilted relative to each other, and planets move faster when nearer the Sun. Distances are to scale; planet markers are enlarged so they can be seen. Starting positions approximate those on 1 January 2000.
